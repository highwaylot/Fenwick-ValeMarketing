1Concept and voice

The sign a driver already knows how to read

A trucker knows one sign better than his own signature: the green plate with white letters and an arrow. He reads a hundred of them a shift without deciding to. FleetCleared cannot borrow the government's authority, and should not want to. It can borrow that reflex. So this is the version a sign painter at a Texas truck stop would make of a guide sign: slab letters no highway department has ever used, on the green every driver's eye already tracks, with one arrow. It hangs at the pump, on the lounge wall, on the back of the founder's pickup. The phone at 11 p.m. shows the same plate in the same proportion, and at night the ground goes dark while the sign stays green, because that is what a reflective sign does.

Round 2 adds the thing under the sign. Every painted sign in a truck stop has a board beneath it: the diner's prices, the shower list, the scale's hours. Ruled rows, a number, a check. That board is how this concept learned the Dispatcher's lesson. The plate is allowed to be warm. The board is not allowed to be anything but true: what's done, what isn't, what date, how many of ten.

The voice is short lines a tired man can read from the cab. "Got a DOT letter? Start here." "No robocalls, ever." "7 of 10 yes." In Spanish it is the same person speaking, not a translator: "¿Te llegó carta del DOT? Empieza aquí." "Sin robollamadas, nunca." Warm, never cute. No cartoon truck, no mascot, no flag. The reader drives for a living, and we remember his order without making a thing of it.

Kept from today's brand

  • Deep greenMoved three shades toward guide-sign green (#0f4c3a → #0d5c3c). Same family, now a color a driver has a memory for.
  • Fleet / ClearedTwo-tone wordmark: "Fleet" in ink, "Cleared" in green.
  • The standing lines"No robocalls, ever." "Sources on every page." "Not affiliated with the FMCSA or the U.S. Department of Transportation." "Need to inspect our claims?" Kept word for word, plus the Spanish line already in use: "Sin robollamadas, nunca."
  • Light and darkKept as day and night, now a switch the reader can reach, beside the language.

Replaced, and why

  • Gradient shieldRetired. Shields drift toward badges; gradients die in vinyl. The plate prints in two colors and cannot be mistaken for law enforcement.
  • Off-white #f4f6f4Enamel cream #f3ead6: a painted board, not a dashboard.
  • Archivo / Source SansAlfa Slab One for the painted words; Overpass, a Google face derived from Highway Gothic, for everything a person reads.
  • "Plain answers before a bad inspection finds you.""Got a DOT letter? Start here." The old line leads with the inspection and quietly threatens. The new one meets Marcus with the letter in his hand.
  • Tilted guide-card on socialThe plate, on a fixed grid, with a written grammar. One asset instead of a layout trick.

  1. Corner: the series line and the small mark. Top left, 70% size, letterspaced capitals: "From the guides," "Spot the fake letter," "Roadcheck week · May," "De las guías." Top right, the small mark at 11% of the card width. These two never move.
  2. The plate holds four words or fewer. A question or a promise, slab capitals, on the brand card and the carousel cover only. On a green card the plate inverts: white plate, green letters. Never a number alone in the plate, never a date, never a dollar sign.
  3. The line: number first, then the fact. Alfa Slab One at 1.75× body, twelve words or fewer, sentence case, one period. Numerals, never spelled out: "16 things," "72 hours," "Day 15." Days are stated as "Day 15," never "15 days left." Nothing counts down.
  4. Relief: one plain sentence, then one that lowers the pulse. Overpass, body size. The second sentence is the brand's manners: "disorganized, not unsafe," "the fix is paperwork," "you have time." A card without a relief line is not finished.
  5. Standing lines, fixed positions. Bottom left: the URL in slab, then where to inspect the claim ("Sources on every page," "the terms are one page"). Bottom right, bold: "No robocalls, ever." and under it, small, the non-affiliation line. On the brand card, where the plate already says the promise, the right side carries the non-affiliation line alone, in full.
  6. Green or cream. Green card for facts and for consultants. Cream card for comparisons (the fake letter) and for the brand card, so the plate can appear inside it. Rust appears only in the "junk" column, and scam words appear only inside quotation marks there.
  7. A Spanish card differs in nothing but the words. Same grid, same plate, same fonts, same colors. "Sin robollamadas, nunca." sits where "No robocalls, ever." sits; "No afiliados a la FMCSA ni al USDOT" under it. No flag, no accent color, no ornament. The sentences are written in Spanish, not translated from the English, so they come out shorter.
  8. The caption carries what changes. Dates, that year's focus area, the source link, any hashtag. The card carries only what will still be true in two years. Sign-off in the caption: "Need to inspect our claims? Sources on every page."

Borrowed reflex, owned detail, repeated grammar

We are not asking a trucker to learn a new shape. A green plate with white letters and an arrow is the most-rehearsed visual pattern in his working life; he has processed it tens of thousands of times without attention, which is the only way anything gets stored by a man who is tired. What we add to that pathway is small and odd enough to stick: slab letters where there should be Highway Gothic, a cream board where there should be a steel post, an arrow that points at a web address instead of an exit. Three months after he saw the plate on a pickup tailgate in a Love's lot, the cue that fires is not our name. It is his own moment, a letter in his hand at 11 p.m. and the question "where do I go." The arrow already answered that once, at the fuel island, in the only grammar a driver reads without trying.

Round 2 adds the part that makes a brand instead of a logo: repetition without drift. The post grammar means the fortieth card in a Facebook group looks exactly like the first, and the Spanish one looks exactly like the English one, so each exposure lands on the same memory instead of a new one. The phone then shows him the same plate at the same proportion, the green unchanged in the dark, the same four words under it, and under those a board of plain rows that has never tried to sell him anything. Recognition does the work that recall would have failed at, and the board earns the second visit.

7Compliance self-check

Every hard rule, and where it holds

  • ✓Nothing governmental The plate uses a slab serif no highway agency uses, a cream ground and a web address; no route number, exit tab, Interstate shield, seal, eagle, star or stripe anywhere. Green-and-white is roadway furniture, not an agency palette; FMCSA and USDOT are blue and white. No "NOTICE," no "OFFICIAL," no letterhead block. The plan places nothing at weigh stations or inspection sites and nothing on a roadside post; the sticker never says "cleared" alone.
  • ✓The shield stays a brand mark Retired, so there is nothing to drift toward a badge. The plate is a rectangle with a web address on it.
  • ✓No "verified / vetted / trusted / certified / approved / endorsed" Every consultant surface says "stated by the consultant," lists an entity name as a fact, and ends with "FleetCleared is a directory. It doesn't certify, rank or recommend anyone listed." The directory list is ordered by distance and says so. No stars, no ratings, no "top," no sponsored row, no response-time badge.
  • ✓No deadline threats, penalty figures, "action required," "past due," or borrowed urgency Brenda's dates appear once, as rows, as "Day 15" and "Day 60," never as a countdown. 8A note to the founder

Post before you print, print before you build

You liked the social work most, so test that first. It costs nothing and it tells you whether the plate carries a fact, which is the whole bet.

  1. The Facebook pair, this week. Post card 1 (the 16 things) in one Texas trucking group and the same words on today's tilted guide-card in a comparable group, same hour, a day apart. Count saves and shares first, comments second, clicks third. Saves predict a man finding it again at 11 p.m. Run it twice more with cards 2 and 6 before you believe the number.
  2. The carousel, in a Spanish group, with a condition. Post the fake-letter carousel in English in one group and card 4 in a Laredo group the same week. The Spanish card stands alone fine because it is a fact, not an offer. Do not post anything that says "se habla español" about a consultant until one is actually listed; that promise unkept is the first thing Beto's group will screenshot.
  3. The cluster, for free. Before any redesign, put a plain EN | ES switch at the top right of the live site that only swaps the homepage headline and the three "tonight" lines. Watch in Vercel analytics how many taps it gets from Laredo and Houston IPs over a month. If people tap it, Spanish pages are worth writing; if nobody does, you have learned that for nothing.
  4. Five truckers at a fuel island, $60. Only after the posts. Two 12×18 boards from a sign shop: this plate, and today's shield beside the name. Stand them by your tailgate at a Love's or a TA around 6 p.m. Ask five drivers: "What is this for?" "Who put it there?" "Would you expect them to call you?" You want "something for trucking companies" or "a website," not "DOT" or "the state." Two of five saying "DOT" means more cream and less green, and I have told you so here first.
  5. Then the plate. One magnet for the tailgate, $80 to $150. Drive it for a month. Then the lounge board for the one independent truck stop that will hang it. Then, and not before, the cards, the toppers, the postcards. Don't buy the TV network this year.

One more thing. The post grammar box is the most valuable thing on this page and the least exciting. Give it to whoever makes your cards, including future you at midnight, and insist on it. A brand is a sign painted the same way for years.

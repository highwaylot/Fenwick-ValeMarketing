Round 2 · the vibe, made usable

The calm, proven at depth

Round 1 showed four quiet screens. Round 2 shows the crowded ones: a directory Tasha can compare three consultants in, a guide with sixteen rules, the audit check halfway through, a result with ten squares. Same eight colors, same one typeface, same ledger. If the calm only works when there is nothing on the page, it is not calm, it is empty. This page is the test.

The control at the top right of this page is the real thing. EN | ES flips every mockup on the page into Spanish and back. The square flips day and night for the whole page; the same square inside any mockup flips that one frame. Nothing is loaded from outside except Inter.

01Concept and voice

The brand is the layout. Still.

Nothing in the idea changed between rounds. The paper a carrier gets from the government is the ugliest document in his working life, and the sellers who copy it add fear on purpose, so FleetCleared looks like the opposite thing, and the opposite of frightening is not friendly. It is clear. One typeface. A ledger: what it is on the left, what to do on the right, a hairline between rows. Numbers that line up. White space that says nobody here is shouting. One green for a thing that is cleared, one amber for a thing that is due, and no red anywhere, because carriers who fail audits are disorganized, not dangerous. Every piece ends with a small green square, the way a proof ends.

What changed is the standard I held the screens to. The founder said the Dispatcher looked like something a trucker would trust because it looked like a tool and not a brochure: status, number, date, done or not done, nothing persuading. A railway timetable passes that test and so does a ledger, but only if every row is a fact and the page never breaks into prose to sell. So in round 2 the rule for every screen is: could this be pinned to a dispatcher's wall and still be useful? The directory is a list with four filters and a compare panel, not a marketplace. The guide is sixteen numbered rows with the rule beside each. The audit check is ten squares that fill in. The result is a column of squares and two lines of what to do. Where round 1 had a sentence of reassurance, round 2 has a number.

The second change is Spanish. In round 1 Spanish was a line in a table. In round 2 it is a language the whole site speaks, one at a time: a two-state switch sits beside day and night in one small bar at the top right of every screen, and the page flips entirely. Not stacked, not a badge, not a translated footer. The ledger cannot carry two languages per row without becoming the spreadsheet I am trying to avoid, so the Swiss answer is to switch rather than pair. The guard from round 1 still holds everywhere: no seals, no capitals as authority, no blue, no boxes around warnings, no form numbers, and sentences written the way a person talks.

Borrowed, and from whom

  • DispatcherThe standard, not the look. "Status, number, date, done or not done." Every screen below was cut against it: the green-square progress strip in the audit check, the result as a column of squares, the directory rows that state facts and never describe. Also one small thing: "their statement" as the label on anything a consultant claims. I had "self-reported"; theirs is plainer and I took it.
  • BilingualThe switch as a brand object at the top right, thumb-reachable, the same object as the mark, never a globe icon. The idea that whichever language is chosen is line one, not a translation. "Sin robollamadas, nunca." as the Spanish standing line. "Se habla español" as words a listee says about themselves, worn only when true. What I did not take: the two-line paired headline. On a ledger it doubles every row; the Swiss way is one language at a time, flipped by the switch.
  • Sign PainterNothing visual. One lesson from the social work: the post is the words, so the Spanish card differs from the English one by nothing but the words. That was already my rule; theirs confirmed it.

Changed from round 1

  • ControlsA new component, the only one added: the EN | ES and day/night cluster. One bar, one hairline border, the active language filled ink. 32 px on desktop, 40 px on the phone, repeated in the phone footer. Drawn once in section 02 and used on every screen.
  • The amber squareRound 1 used amber only for day counts. In the audit check a "no" is a thing to do, so it gets the amber square. Green means cleared, amber means due, an outline means not yet answered. Still no red.
  • The visor cardKept, and given two editions, EN and ES, with the edition letters printed in the card's corner in the same segmented shape as the switch. Drawn front, back and clipped to a visor in section 05.
  • SocialThree cards became six, plus a 9:16 story, the Facebook cover and the avatar. The grid did not change; the number card and the mailbox card from round 1 are here unchanged except for a word.
  • DensityThree screens round 1 never drew: the directory with filters and a three-way comparison, the guide with its ledger of 16, and the audit check mid-flow with its result. These are the proof the founder asked for.

How it stays a ledger and not a spreadsheet. The filters are a ledger row: label above, value below, a hairline under the value instead of a box. The 16 violations that fail a new entrant audit on their own

Short answer. Sixteen specific violations fail the audit whatever else is in order. Nearly all of them are about paper: a program you didn't join, a file missing a page, an inspection that didn't happen. The list is below in the rule's order, with what to keep for each one.

Most carriers pass. The most common of the 16, by far, is number 5: no random drug and alcohol testing.

06Why it will be remembered

Recognized by shape before it is read, in either language.

A trucker at a fuel island is handed a white card. He does not read the name. He sees two columns of little boxes, a rule down the middle, numbers that line up, a green square, and he notices what is missing: no shouting, no seal, no red. He clips it to the visor because it is useful, and over three months he ticks four boxes. At 11 p.m. in the sleeper, with the audit notice open on the bed, he types his situation into a phone and a page comes up with the same shape: label on the left, answer on the right, a hairline, the square at the end of the headline, and the same closing line he half-remembers from the card. He does not think "FleetCleared." He thinks "this is the one that isn't trying to scare me." His cousin in Laredo, handed the Spanish card at a Saturday class, has the identical experience with different words, and when the two of them compare phones the pages match. Distinctive assets work when they are few, identical, and tied to one promise; here there are five, they never vary across language or density or theme, and the promise is the same on the card, the page, the directory and the envelope: nobody here is going to call you. Round 2 adds the proof that the shape holds when the page is full. A seven-row directory and a ten-square result look like the card because they are built from the same five things. The sellers cannot copy this without giving up the thing that makes them money, which is fear. That is what makes the asset defensible.

07Compliance self-check

Every hard rule, checked against every screen.

  • Nothing governmental. No seal, eagle, star or stripe anywhere. The mark is a ruled square with a green quadrant, a form-field shape, not a badge. No blue token exists. No capitals used as authority: the only capitals on any surface are the 13 px margin labels, the two-letter EN and ES, and the struck-through phrase on the two mailbox cards. No "NOTICE," "OFFICIAL," letterhead. The only rule citations (49 CFR …) appear in grey as sources on the guide and the audit check, never as our authority. Weigh stations and inspection sites get nothing, on purpose.
  • No "verified, vetted, trusted, certified, approved, endorsed." Checked by search across this file: none of those words appears anywhere except negated, here and in one caption. The directory and the card say "lists," "doesn't check their work, rate them or rank them," and print "their statement" beside every consultant claim. No stars, no ratings, no ranks, no "best match"; results are alphabetical. The recruiting card tells consultants there are no reviews.
  • No deadline threats or penalty figures. The only day counts shown are the reader's own (15, 45, 60 on the crisis page, the story and the card), stated as information with the source cited. 08A note to the founder

Build the bar first. Test the card first. Then the directory.

You said this one had good vibes. Here is what I think the vibe is: nothing on the page is asking the reader for anything. That survives density only if the copy stays factual, so the thing to protect is not the layout, it is the habit of writing a number where a sentence would do. Cheap tests, in order:

  1. 1Build the controls cluster now, before any redesign. The theme toggle already exists in your CSS. Add the EN | ES switch beside it, in one bordered bar, top right, even if ES leads to a single Spanish page at first. It is the one thing every concept in this round agreed on, it is an afternoon of work, and it starts teaching the object before the rest of the site changes.
  2. 2Fifty cards, one fuel island, one Saturday. Print the English visor card with clips (under $100). Hand it to drivers fueling off I-35 or I-20. Say one sentence: "Sixteen things that fail a new entrant audit; it clips to the visor." Count who takes it, who reads it before pocketing it, who asks a question. Two weeks later, check whether search traffic matches the card's words. If fewer than one in three read it on the spot, cut the front to the eight most common.
  3. 3Twenty Spanish cards, one Saturday class in Laredo. Same test, Spanish edition, and one question afterward: "¿Hay alguien en Laredo?" The answer tells you the directory demand in Spanish before the directory exists, and whether "EN | ES" or the word "Español" is the right label on the switch. Ask them which they would tap.
  4. 4A Facebook post pair. Same group, a week apart, same time of evening. Post A is today's style: the tilted guide card on a colored field. Post B is the square "16" ledger above. Measure saves and shares, not likes; a save means someone expects to need it. Repeat once in a Spanish group with the Spanish mailbox card.
  5. 5Two office managers, one screenshot. Before the directory is built, show the compare panel above to two people who do Tasha's job at 10-to-20-truck carriers. Ask them to pick one of the three in sixty seconds and say why. If they ask "which one is best," the panel is missing a fact they need, and you should find out which fact rather than add a rating.
  6. 6Five truckers, one question. Show the current homepage and the ledger homepage on your own phone, side by side, five seconds each. Ask only: "Which one is from the government?" If anyone picks the ledger, the Swiss direction has a problem and you should know before spending on print.
  7. 7Then the site, in this order. The cluster. The homepage template: Inter, the ledger, the four rows, the closing line. The audit check and its result, since they are the two screens where the design most changes the product. The guides' frame, content untouched. The directory last, when it is real.

One caution, the same as round 1 and more so now. Swiss restraint fails when the copy is weak, because there is nothing to hide behind, and a directory with seven listings is a lot of copy you do not write. Give consultants a ledger to fill in, four fields and a sentence, rather than a free text box. The form is the design; what they type into it is what Tasha compares.

Reviews: what you can ask customers, and what gets you fined

Fenwick and Vale · Cocoa Beach, Florida · October 2026 · Sources are linked where the numbers are.

Two sets of rules cover your Google reviews: federal law, and Google's own policy. Google's rules are stricter. If you follow Google's, you'll satisfy both. Here they are on one page.

The law

The Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews took effect on October 21, 2024 (FTC; 16 CFR Part 465). It bans:

  • Fake reviews, including ones written by AI.
  • Paying for a review that has to be positive (or negative).
  • Reviews by you, your staff or your relatives that don't say so.
  • A review site you control but present as independent.
  • Threats to get a review removed, or showing a hand-picked set of reviews as if it were all of them.

The penalty is up to $53,088 per violation (FTC, 2025), and the FTC is holding that number for 2026 (FTC 2026 notice). One fake review is one violation.

The rule has a safe harbor. Asking your customers in general for reviews is fine; the rule exempts reviews that come from "generalized solicitations" (16 CFR 465.2). Asking only the happy ones, called gating, isn't banned by the rule itself. But in July 2025 the FTC charged a company called NextMed with, among other things, "selectively soliciting positive reviews from satisfied customers," and the company paid $150,000 (FTC). Don't gate.

The FTC has brought a handful of cases so far, mostly against larger companies, and none yet under this rule by name. For a local business the everyday risk isn't the fine, it's Google: removed reviews, a frozen profile, a public warning on your listing.

Google's rules

Google goes further. It bans any incentive for a review: no discount, no gift, no raffle. It bans asking only happy customers, pressuring people while they're in your shop, telling them what to write, setting review quotas for staff, and reviews from current or former employees or contractors (Google's review policy; Google Business Profile Help).

What happens when you break them: the reviews come down and don't come back; your profile can be blocked from receiving new reviews for a while; Google can post a public warning on your profile that fake reviews were removed; and the account can be suspended (Google). A sudden flood of reviews is also a risk. "Receiving too many Google reviews too fast" ranks 12th among suspension risks in Whitespark's 2026 survey of local search experts (Whitespark).

What you can do

  • Ask every customer, the same way, within a day of the job, by text or email.
  • Use Google's own review link or QR code. Your profile gives you both under "Ask for reviews" (Google). Put the QR code on receipts and at the counter.
  • Reply to every review within a week, good or bad.
  • Keep a steady trickle. Don't blast an old customer list.
  • Leave Yelp alone. Yelp's guidelines say businesses "should never ask customers to write reviews," and Yelp posts public alerts on businesses caught paying for them (Yelp; Yelp Consumer Alerts).

A real example. A receptionist texts a patient the next day: "If you were happy with our office, please review us on Google. It should only take a minute." A person texting one customer from the office phone is the safe way to ask, but drop the "if you were happy" condition, because Google's policy bars selectively asking for positive reviews. The clean version: "Thanks for coming in today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps a small office. Here's the link."

What to expect

The review software company GatherUp reports about 20 reviews per 100 requests sent by text, 15 per 100 by email, and 26 per 100 when both are used (GatherUp). That's vendor data, but it's the best there is. A shop that finishes 15 jobs a month will see three or four new reviews a month. That's normal. It adds up.

It's worth doing. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 47% said they won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, 68% want four stars or more, 32% look for reviews from the last two weeks, and 89% expect the owner to reply (BrightLocal).

The short version

Ask everyone. Pay no one. Pick no one. Reply to everyone. Use Google's link.

Ten signs a marketing agency is taking you for a ride

Fenwick and Vale · Cocoa Beach, Florida · October 2026 · Sources are linked where the numbers are.

Most agencies are honest. The ones that aren't use the same few tricks, and Google and the Better Business Bureau have written them down. Here are ten. One is a reason to ask questions. Two or more is a reason to walk.

1. They guarantee a ranking

Nobody can promise you a spot on Google. Google says so in its own guide for business owners who hire help (Google Ads: working with third parties), and its rules forbid agencies from guaranteeing placement (Google Ads policy). The BBB puts a "guaranteed #1 ranking" first on its list of marketing scams, and says the person offering it is "either lying or about to get your site penalized" (BBB).

2. They say they're calling from Google

"We work with Google." "Verify now or lose your listing." A robocall about your listing. Google doesn't make those calls (BBB). Anyone who really works at Google has an @google.com email address (Google). Google's rules also ban claiming to be a "Google Partner" without the badge, and selling the free Business Profile as if it cost money (Google Ads policy).

3. A twelve-month contract

The BBB names "a twelve-month lock-in paired with monthly reports full of charts that never mention leads, phone calls, or sales" as a warning sign (BBB). Month to month with 30 days' notice is normal. Ninety days' notice is not (Top Growth Marketing).

4. Your domain is in their name

If the agency registered yourbusiness.com under its own account, it owns your address. Leave, and your site goes dark, or your customers land on theirs. Web consultants have warned about this for years (Michael Carusi). Ask who the registrant is. It should be you, with your own login.

5. The Google accounts aren't yours

Google tells owners to insist on a separate Google Ads account in their own name, and says you have a right to know your clicks, impressions and total cost (Google). The same goes for your Business Profile, Analytics and Meta accounts. You should be the admin from day one (Top Growth Marketing).

6. Reports with no calls or leads in them

Impressions, followers and "reach" are not customers. A report should say how many calls, forms and reviews came in. Before you pay, ask what will be done each month and how it will be measured (BBB).

7. A secret method

"A proprietary system we can't reveal" is on the BBB's list (BBB). There is no secret. Google publishes its rules. Anyone who won't explain what they do is hiding how little it is.

8. Sign today

An expiring deadline. A hard push to sign on the first call. A large payment up front (BBB). A fair deal is still fair next week. For a website build, 50% to start and the rest at launch is normal (Penly).

9. A bill for something you never ordered

Fake invoices are one of the most common scams aimed at businesses. The BBB says the usual ones are for "office supplies, website or domain hosting services, and directory listings" (BBB). Before you pay any bill for your website, check who actually hosts it and where the domain is registered. Pay those two, and nobody else.

10. No prices until you get on a call

If you have to book a sales call to learn what anything costs, the price depends on how much they think you can pay. In a survey of 2,185 business buyers, 57% named missing prices as one of the top reasons not to buy (TrustRadius, via VentureBeat). When I checked in October 2026, about 20 agencies listed Brevard addresses and two of them published prices. The rest may be good. But you should know the number before the pitch.

What to do instead

Get the agreement in writing, in plain language. Ask for references from businesses like yours, and call them (BBB). Make sure the domain, the site and every account are in your name before the first payment. If you've already been taken, report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or BBB Scam Tracker.

When you don't need me: if your current agency passes all ten, you have a good one. Keep them. You don't need Fenwick and Vale.
